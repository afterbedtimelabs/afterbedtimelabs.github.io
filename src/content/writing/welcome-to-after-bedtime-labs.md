After Bedtime Labs is a home for the things I build outside the day job: software, AI experiments, and games. I’m Isaac, a husband, dad, and software engineer. The name is fairly literal. This is work that fits into the hours in between.

The projects vary, but they share a useful starting point: a small question that becomes more interesting once there is something to try. Can a desktop companion be useful and pleasant to have around? What happens when equipment builds your deck? How much unease can a quiet walk carry?

This site gives those questions a place to live, alongside notes about the decisions behind them.

## Three things on the workbench

**[Riku](/projects/riku/)** is a desktop companion experiment. Developed under the name Navi, it brings together local conversation, voice, memory, and everyday tools. There is a lot to learn in making those pieces feel like one coherent companion: how it responds, what it remembers, and when it should simply leave you to your work. Riku is the public-facing name here; the existing [Navi Gmail Monitor information](/navi/) and [privacy policy](/navi/privacy/) keep their established names and addresses.

**[Dungeon Deckbuilder](/projects/dungeon-deckbuilder/)** starts with a rule: your equipment determines your deck. A new piece of gear changes your available cards; investing in an existing piece deepens a build. The prototype has six playable classes and a short run for playtesting. The next questions are about clarity and meaningful choices—whether players can understand a tradeoff before committing to it.

**[Midnight Chiba](/projects/midnight-chiba/)** takes a quieter direction. It is a slow-burn walking game through a Japanese neighborhood after dark, from the station toward home. The work is in the spaces between landmarks: a narrow lane, an exposed field, a warm storefront against an otherwise dark road. Environment, sound, and pacing have to carry the evening.

All three are works in progress. Their project pages distinguish what exists now from what is still being explored, and will point to public builds when those are available.

## A notebook, not just a finished-project shelf

The useful part of a project is often a decision that gets lost by the time there is a release: why an interface changed, why a mechanic was removed, or why a simpler approach worked better.

That is what the writing section is for. I want each technical note to have something concrete at its center—a problem, an attempt, and an honest account of what happened. When there is a result worth sharing, it belongs next to the project. When something is still an outline, it stays on the drafting table.

There is no fixed publishing schedule. A small useful note is enough.
